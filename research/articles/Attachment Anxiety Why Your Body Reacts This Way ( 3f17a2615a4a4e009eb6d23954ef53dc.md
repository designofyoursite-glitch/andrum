## 1. What is attachment theory?

In plain human language: attachment theory is a way to describe how we experience closeness and safety with the people who matter to us.

Why one person can calmly handle “See you tomorrow,” while someone else gets thrown into a storm by “Seen… but no reply.”

This isn’t about being “weak” or “dramatic.” It’s about how your nervous system reads connection — as a steady anchor, or as something that could disappear any second.

## 2. Important note: we don’t diagnose

A few important things up front:

- We **don’t diagnose** and we don’t decide “what your attachment style is.”
- We don’t treat this model as the only truth.
- You get to **take what resonates and leave what doesn’t**.

We use attachment language as a **useful map**:

- to name what’s actually happening in your relational nervous system
- to choose supportive tools for specific states

And one more thing that many people find relieving: even if you don’t like the word “attachment” — that’s okay.

Andrum practices work on a **physical level**. They help reduce nervous system overload, bring you back into your body, and restore clarity — no matter what you call your experience.

## 3. Common attachment patterns

This is simplified. Not a diagnosis, not a label, not a life sentence.

And a key idea (often emphasized by modern authors, including Jessica Fern in *Polysecure*): attachment style isn’t a fixed “stamp.” It’s a **dynamic pattern** that can shift across relationships and soften or intensify depending on stress, lived experience, and how much safety exists in the relationship.

### Anxious

It’s like you have a built-in radar for distance: a pause, a delayed reply, a change in tone — and your body immediately concludes, “The connection is in danger.”

So you want contact, clarity, reassurance — not for drama, but so your nervous system can finally exhale.

### Avoidant

Closeness can feel risky: “too close = too vulnerable.”

A common strategy is self-reliance and control — asking for less, showing less need, moving into your head or into doing, so you don’t have to feel dependent on someone else.

### Secure

There’s an inner sense of ground: you can be close and still stay yourself.

If anxiety or conflict shows up, there’s usually a basic trust that it can be talked through and repaired — without losing yourself in the process.

### Mixed (anxious–avoidant)

A pendulum: you crave closeness — and when it becomes real, fear rises and you want distance.

It can look like a cycle: closeness → overload → distance → longing/anxiety → closeness again.

### A small Polysecure-style note: “security” doesn’t have to come from one person

In *Polysecure*, there’s a very supportive idea: safety can be “built” from more than one source.

For many people, security is layered: your relationship with yourself, close friends, community, therapy, somatic practices. When support is distributed, anxiety in relationships has less room to take over.

## 4. Anxious attachment

Anxious attachment is a pattern where connection with an important person can feel **unstable** and **easy to lose**.

Inside, there may be a rule like: “closeness = safety, distance = danger.” So even small uncertainty (a pause, a delayed reply, a tone shift) can register as threat.

Important: Andrum was created to support women who recognize symptoms of **attachment anxiety** — especially in relationships.

## 5. Where it comes from (roots)

Most often, anxious attachment grows in environments where connection felt **unpredictable**: warmth and responsiveness were “there sometimes, gone sometimes.”

Your nervous system learns to:

- “turn up the signal” to be heard
- search for proof of connection
- treat uncertainty as risk

This can also be intensified by cultural conditioning like “be easy,” “don’t ask for too much,” “don’t get angry.”

When direct needs feel shameful, the need for closeness can come out sideways — as anxiety, control, and self-criticism.

## 6. How it shows up: mind, body, behavior

One of the most common experiences people describe is: “I know I’m spiraling — but I can’t stop.”

That makes sense with attachment anxiety: the body fires first, and then the thoughts arrive to explain the alarm.

### Mind

- catastrophizing: “they’re leaving,” “I don’t matter,” “something is wrong with me”
- reading between the lines, replaying conversations, hunting for hidden meanings
- loops: like your brain is chewing the same thought again and again

### Body

- tight chest or stomach ("like a vice"), heat, shaking, nausea
- shallow breathing, insomnia
- spikes of activation followed by exhaustion

### Behavior

This isn’t “you’re toxic” or “you’re weak.” It’s your nervous system trying to get back to safety.

- checking for signals (texts, social media)
- impulsive texts/calls (and then: “why did I send that…”)
- protest behaviors: sharpness, hurt, ultimatums — or the opposite: going silent

## 7. What anxious attachment is not (dropping shame)

- It’s not your “personality” and it’s not a “defect.”
- It’s not proof that you’re “too much.”
- It’s not always “love”: often it’s **fear of losing connection**, experienced by the body as threat.

And if you’ve ever thought, “I feel crazy in love” — that’s sadly a common part of this pattern.

Nothing is “wrong” with you. Your system is flipping into survival mode — and it can be supported.

## 8. What helps: directions for work

These aren’t magic tips. They’re directions that create real change over time.

### Nervous system regulation

Goal: triggers get quieter, shorter, and recovery gets faster.

- sleep, food, movement (boring but foundational)
- somatic practices and breathwork as a skill (small doses, consistently)

### Working with thoughts

Goal: reduce catastrophizing and come back to reality.

- separate facts from interpretations
- notice automatic “stories” and test them

### Boundaries and self-grounding

Goal: relationships aren’t your only source of stability.

- return your focus to your life: purpose, people, meaning
- strengthen self-worth and the right to have needs

### Relationship communication

Goal: ask for closeness directly, without control or blame.

Examples:

- “Being in touch helps me feel grounded. Can you text when you’re free?”
- “If you’re busy, a quick ‘later’ helps me not spiral.”
- “Pauses can trigger me. Can we check in — are we okay?”

## 9. When to seek extra support

If attachment anxiety leads to frequent panic, destructive conflict, self-harm/substance use, hopelessness, or depressive episodes — it’s best to involve a mental health professional.
