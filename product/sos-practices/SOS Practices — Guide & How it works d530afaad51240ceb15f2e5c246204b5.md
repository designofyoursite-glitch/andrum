# SOS Practices — Guide & How it works

This document is the practical guide for the **SOS Practices** collection: how to choose a practice, how to use it in the moment, and how the method works.

## **What are the SOS practices?**

The SOS kit contains **7 short audio practices**, each **5–10 minutes** long.

They were designed for acute moments — when the body is already in the wave and the mind is trying to catch up.

Each practice works with a specific state: anxiety after a triggering message, thoughts that won’t stop cycling, the shame of feeling “not enough,” jealousy, the desperate urge to reach out, emotional numbness, and the heat of anger.

A practice is not a meditation. It is a structured intervention: a specific breathing rhythm combined with affirmations that land *after* the body begins to settle.

*These practices don’t ask you to feel differently. They give the nervous system a different signal — and let the feeling follow.*

## **How to use the kit**

There is no right order. There is no programme to follow. The practices are tools — you reach for the one that fits the state you are in right now.

### **How to breathe**

- **Inhale through the nose — always.**
- **Exhale through the nose or mouth** (choose what feels easier in the moment).
- Keep the breath soft and unforced. If anything feels uncomfortable or you get dizzy, return to natural breathing and pause.
- **Your position doesn’t matter:** you can do the practices **standing, sitting, or lying down**, and some can be done **in motion/while walking** (for example, on a walk).

### **In an acute moment**

1. Find the state that matches what you feel.
2. Start the practice.
3. Before each practice, you can read a short description and recommendations for how to do it. But if you’re in a very acute state, you can also just press play and begin — the voice will guide you through everything.

If you can, find a quiet place. But most practices work anywhere — in a bathroom, on a walk, sitting in your car. Practice 05 is specifically designed for movement: earbuds in, start walking.

### **A few things worth knowing**

- You do not need to feel the practice “working” immediately. Sometimes the shift is subtle — a small softening, a slightly longer breath. That is enough.
- It is normal for the mind to wander. Return to the count or the voice. Each return is the practice.
- If a practice feels like too much, return to natural breathing and come back when ready. There is no pressure.
- Each practice can be repeated as many times as needed.

<aside>
🫶

**A note about safety and support**

If you feel dizzy or overwhelmed, return to natural breathing and pause the practice.

These practices support emotional regulation in the moment. They are not a substitute for therapy.

If what you’re carrying feels too heavy to hold alone, please reach out to a mental health professional.

</aside>

### **Contraindications (please read)**

If any of the points below apply to you, please consult a medical professional before doing breathwork practices.

- You are pregnant
- You have a heart condition, high blood pressure, or a history of stroke
- You have epilepsy or a history of seizures
- You have glaucoma or a retinal condition
- You are currently experiencing severe asthma symptoms or significant breathing difficulties
- You have recently had surgery, injury, or a condition where breath retention or strong breathing could be unsafe
- You have a history of panic attacks that are easily triggered by changes in breathing (start gently, stop if symptoms intensify)

If you’re unsure, start with a gentler practice, keep the breath natural, and pause at any time.

## **How this works inside**

This section is for when you want to understand what is happening *inside you* when the wave hits — and why the practices can help. It’s not required reading, but it can give you a deeper understanding of the physiological processes in your body.

### **What is happening in your body**

When something triggers you in a relationship — a cold reply, a silence, a tone of voice — the amygdala fires before the thinking brain has a chance to respond.

In that moment, the sympathetic nervous system activates. Adrenaline and cortisol flood the body. Heart rate rises. Breathing becomes shallow and fast. The prefrontal cortex — responsible for perspective, reasoning, and self-regulation — goes partially offline.

That’s why it can feel impossible to “be reasonable” in the moment, even when you understand exactly what is happening.

This is why you cannot think your way out of the wave. Understanding lives in the cortex. The reaction lives in the body.

### **Why breath is the direct line**

Breath is the only function of the body that operates both automatically and voluntarily.

When you slow your breath deliberately — particularly when you extend the exhale — you activate the parasympathetic nervous system through the vagus nerve. Stimulating it sends one clear signal throughout the system: the threat has passed.

Specifically, slow rhythmic breathing:

- **Lowers heart rate** — the extended exhale lengthens the interval between heartbeats, which the body reads as safety
- **Reduces cortisol** — the stress hormone begins to drop within minutes
- **Quiets the amygdala** — the threat-detection system receives the signal that danger has passed
- **Brings the prefrontal cortex back online** — reasoning, perspective and choice gradually return
- **Interrupts the feedback loop** — where physical tension generates anxious thought, which generates more tension

### **Why affirmations come second**

Words spoken to a highly activated nervous system do not land. The same words spoken to a nervous system that has found even 20% more calm reach a completely different place.

This is why every practice follows the same sequence: breath first, affirmations after.

### **Why each practice is different**

Each state has a different physiology, and each technique is chosen to address it directly:

- Acute panic needs a fast discharge
- Racing thoughts need structure
- Shame needs warmth and expansion
- Jealousy needs steady reassurance
- The urge to reach out needs metabolisation
- Freeze needs gentleness
- Anger needs a physical exit

### **What changes over time**

Over weeks and months of regular use, these practices create new neural pathways. The reactive pattern does not disappear — but the path back to regulation gets shorter, and the reflex to reach for the breath becomes faster than the reflex to reach for the phone.

*You are not calming down. You are teaching the nervous system that it can survive this moment — and that you will be there to help it.*
