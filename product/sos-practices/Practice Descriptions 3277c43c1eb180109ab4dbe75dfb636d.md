# Practice Descriptions

Each practice is available as an audio track. Below is what to expect before you press play — the context, the technique, key safety notes, and (optionally) a gentle psychological lens for the state you’re in.

# **01  When Anxiety Hits After a Message or Conversation**

*"When you can't think your way to safety, you can breathe your way there."*

**CONTEXT**

A message, a pause in response, a cold tone, or something left unsaid hit harder than you expected. Your body tightens, your breath becomes shallow, panic rises, and it feels like something went wrong. Thoughts start racing. Anxiety comes in waves.

**Key goal:** To bring support back into the body, reduce nervous system reactivity, gently come out of the trigger, and feel grounded again.

**When to use:**

- Right after receiving a triggering message or conversation
- Ideally within 10 minutes of activation
- Can also be used daily as a stabilising ritual

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Physiological Sigh → 4–6 Breathing**

**Step 1 — Physiological Sigh (2–3 rounds)**

- Inhale through the nose
- Take a small second inhale to 'top up' — as if filling the lungs a little more
- Long, slow exhale through the mouth

**Step 2 — 4–6 Breathing (4–5 minutes)**

- Inhale through the nose for 4 counts
- Exhale slowly for 6 counts (through the mouth or nose, whichever feels easier)
- Keep the count gentle — let it hold you, not control you

**Note:** This practice is safe for most people. If you feel lightheaded during the physiological sigh, return to normal breathing and go straight to 4–6. Do not use extended breath holds if you are pregnant or have a heart condition.

**Why it works:**

- The physiological sigh discharges the sharp edge of activation quickly — it is the fastest known way to lower acute physiological stress
- The longer exhale in 4–6 breathing stimulates the vagus nerve, signalling to the nervous system: the threat has passed, you are safe
- Counting gently anchors attention in the present without feeling rigid or forced

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

In this state, the nervous system often treats *uncertainty as danger*. A message or silence can quickly become a story: “something is wrong,” “I’m losing them,” “I need to fix this right now.”

This isn’t you being irrational. It’s your system trying to restore safety through connection.

**A few keys to notice**

- What am I afraid is happening right now — and what do I actually *know*?
- What do I want to do impulsively (text, call, explain, check) — and what is the need underneath that?
- If I felt 10% safer, what would I do differently in the next 10 minutes?

**Small practice (1 minute, after you finish listening)**

Write one sentence:

- “Right now, my body feels ***, and what I need is*** .”

# **02  When Thoughts Are Racing and Won't Stop**

*"When you can't think your way to safety, you can breathe your way there."*

**CONTEXT**

Your mind is running at full speed. Maybe you're replaying a conversation, or imagining how something might go. Maybe you've been trying to stop the thoughts — and somehow that's only made them louder. There is a loop, and you are stuck inside it.

**Key goal:** To interrupt the cycle of obsessive thinking, return attention to the body, and create enough space to put the spiral down — at least for now.

**When to use:**

- When thoughts are cycling and won't stop
- Before sleep when the mind won't quiet down
- After a triggered moment when the body has calmed but the mind is still running
- Any time mental overdrive makes it impossible to be present

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Box Breathing (4–4–4–4)**

Box breathing uses four equal phases to create a structure the mind can follow:

- Inhale for 4 counts
- Hold for 4 counts
- Exhale for 4 counts
- Hold for 4 counts

Let each phase be even and steady. When a thought comes in, you don't have to push it away — just return to the count.

**Note:** Box breathing is generally very safe. If breath holds feel uncomfortable, shorten them to 2 counts or remove them entirely and use slow 4–6 breathing instead.

**Why it works:**

- The symmetrical rhythm creates a 'container' the mind can hold — giving the relentless thinking something to do other than spiral
- Breath holds produce a mild, controlled CO₂ rise that gently interrupts anxious neural loops
- The structured count redirects cognitive resources away from rumination without requiring willpower

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

Racing thoughts often try to create control when you don’t feel safe. The mind replays, predicts, reviews — not because it likes suffering, but because it’s searching for certainty.

This state is not “overthinking as a personality trait.” It’s a regulation strategy.

**A few keys to notice**

- What question is my mind trying to answer right now?
- If I had to name the emotion under the thoughts (fear, grief, shame, longing), what would it be?
- What would I do if I didn’t have to solve this today?

**Small practice (2 minutes, after you finish listening)**

- Write the loop in one line: “My mind keeps saying: ___.”
- Then add: “A kinder truth I can live with today: ___.”

# **03  When I Feel Small, Wrong, or 'Not Enough'**

*"You don't have to earn your way back to yourself. You're already there."*

**CONTEXT**

Shame and guilt rise, along with a deep sense of inadequacy. An inner voice whispers — quietly or loudly: 'I got it wrong. I am wrong. I'm not enough.' The body contracts. There is a desire to hide or disappear. This may come after a moment of emotional reactivity, after a mistake, or simply from nowhere at all.

**Key goal:** To gently restore a sense of inner worth, soften what has tightened, quiet the inner critic without fighting it, and reconnect with dignity.

**When to use:**

- When shame or self-criticism rises after a conversation, a mistake, or an emotional reaction
- When the inner voice is loud and unkind
- As a regular morning practice to begin the day from a place of self-connection
- Any time you feel the need to be gentler with yourself

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Heart-Centred Coherent Breathing (4–4)**

Place your hands gently on your chest (optional but recommended).

- Inhale for 4 counts, imagining the breath moving into the heart space — not just the lungs
- Exhale for 4 counts, letting the chest area soften on the out-breath
- Keep the rhythm even and unhurried — equal in and equal out

If the inner voice is still active, let it be. We are not trying to silence it. We are giving the body a different experience.

**Note:** There are no contraindications for this practice. It is gentle enough for any time of day. If placing hands on the chest feels uncomfortable, rest them on your lap instead.

**Why it works:**

- Shame physically contracts the chest and drops the posture — this breath gently reopens the heart area, counteracting the collapse response directly in the body
- The equal inhale and exhale creates warmth and expansion without further sedating a state that is already withdrawn
- Coherent breathing (approximately 0.1 Hz) has been shown to restore heart rate variability — a physiological marker of emotional resilience

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

Shame often says: “I am the problem.” It shrinks the body and makes connection feel risky. Under shame there is usually a tender need: to be accepted, to be safe, to belong.

This practice doesn’t argue with the inner critic. It helps you return to the part of you that is still here — even while the critic is loud.

**A few keys to notice**

- If my shame had one message, what is it?
- Whose voice does it resemble?
- What would I say to a friend who felt exactly this?

**Small practice (3 minutes, after you finish listening)**

Complete the sentence:

- “I’m not ‘wrong.’ I’m having a moment of ***., and I need***.”

*Morning variation: This practice works beautifully as a daily reset at the start of the day, even without a specific trigger. The 4–4 rhythm is gentle enough to begin any day from a place of self-connection.*

# **04  When Jealousy Rises and You're Scared of Losing Them**

*"Beneath the fear of losing someone, there is almost always a longing to be deeply seen."*

**CONTEXT**

The body is tense. Breathing is short. There is a knot somewhere inside. Fear arises — of being replaced, forgotten, or found lacking. Thoughts like 'he'll pull away,' 'I'm not important,' or 'she is more than me' loop with urgency. Anxious attachment becomes painfully activated.

**Key goal:** To calm the body, reduce the intensity of jealousy, and restore a sense of inner safety and self-worth that is not dependent on anyone else's attention.

**When to use:**

- When jealousy spikes suddenly
- When thoughts of comparison or replacement loop
- During mild to moderate triggers — best used in a quiet space where you can feel your body
- Before responding to a situation that has triggered jealousy, to ensure you respond from clarity

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Extended Exhale Breathing (4–6, or 4–8 for strong activation)**

- Inhale through the nose for 4 counts
- Exhale slowly for 6 counts (through the mouth or nose)
- Keep the pace slow and steady throughout

**For very intense activation:**

Extend the exhale to 8 counts (4–8) for a stronger parasympathetic response. You can layer a silent phrase: on the inhale, 'I am safe' — on the exhale, 'even if I feel scared.'

**Note:** This practice is safe for all. If breathing at 4–8 feels strained, return to 4–6. Do not force the exhale — it should feel long and releasing, not effortful.

**Why it works:**

- Jealousy is high sympathetic activation with an abandonment signal underneath — the longer exhale tells the body: the threat has passed, I am safe right now
- It reduces the internal 'I'm about to lose something' urgency without forcing calm or denying the feeling
- Each extended exhale provides a steady, repeatable reassurance cue that the nervous system can learn over time

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

Jealousy is often a protest: “Please don’t leave me.” It can look like anger or control, but underneath is fear of loss and a longing to be chosen.

This practice is not here to make you “not jealous.” It’s here to reduce the emergency signal so you can respond from self-respect.

**A few keys to notice**

- What am I afraid I will lose right now?
- What story am I telling about myself in this moment (not enough, replaceable, invisible)?
- What would reassurance from *me to me* sound like?

**Small practice (2 minutes, after you finish listening)**

Write two lines:

- “What I’m really longing for is ___.”
- “One way I can give myself a little of that today is ___.”

# **05  When You're Desperate to Reach Out — Let's Walk**

*"You don't have to hold still to find stillness. Sometimes you find it one step at a time."*

**CONTEXT**

Something is burning inside you — a pull toward the phone, the urge to explain, to fix, to make sure everything is still okay. Adrenaline is up. It feels urgent. The body has somewhere it wants to go, and the mind is still deciding whether sending that message is actually a good idea.

**Key goal:** To release excess energy through movement, reduce the intensity of the impulse, discharge tension from the body, and return the ability to choose — rather than react.

**When to use:**

- When your hand is already reaching for the phone
- When the urgency to send a message feels overwhelming
- Best used with headphones during a 5–10 minute walk outside
- An ideal emergency practice before sending a message you might later regret

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Rhythmic Walking Breath (4 steps in · 6 steps out)**

Start walking at a natural pace. Earbuds in. Let the feet take over first.

- Inhale through the nose for 4 steps
- Exhale slowly for 6 steps (through the mouth or nose)
- Keep it natural — if your stride changes, let the breath adjust with it

**No walk available?**

This pattern works just as well pacing indoors — even 10–15 steps back and forth. The key is movement combined with the extended exhale. The body needs somewhere to go with this energy.

**Note:** There are no contraindications. If walking outdoors is not possible, pacing in a small space is equally effective.

**Why it works:**

- Movement metabolises adrenaline — this is what the body was designed for when activated for 'fight or flight'
- The rhythmic walking pattern creates a 'doing something' that redirects the impulse without suppressing it
- The longer exhale gradually activates the parasympathetic nervous system, signalling: you are not in danger, you can slow down

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

The urge to reach out is often the attachment system trying to restore connection *fast*. In that urgency, the body is leading — not because you’re weak, but because it learned that connection equals safety.

Movement helps metabolise the urgency so the choice can return.

**A few keys to notice**

- What do I want to get from reaching out: reassurance, closeness, certainty, repair?
- If I reach out from panic, what usually happens?
- If I wait 20 minutes, what becomes more true?

**Small practice (after the walk, 2 minutes)**

If you still want to message, write a draft starting with:

- “I want to reach out because I’m feeling ***, and what I actually need is*** .”

Then decide whether to send it now or later.

# **06  When I Freeze and Feel Nothing**

*"The body doesn't disappear in freeze. It just goes very, very quiet. And quiet can be reached."*

**CONTEXT**

Emotions seem to have switched off. The body feels heavy — or completely empty. It is hard to feel anger, joy, even anxiety. The world has gone muted. There is a flatness where feeling used to be. You know something happened, but there is nothing there.

**Key goal:** To gently thaw the body, restore sensation and warmth, and come back from the 'I disappear' state — with patience and without any pressure to feel more than is present.

**When to use:**

- When you feel numb, empty, or disconnected from yourself
- After a strong trigger has passed and left nothing behind
- Can be done sitting or lying down, in a calm and quiet moment
- Go slowly — freeze requires gentleness above everything else

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Dirga — Three-Part Breath**

- Inhale slowly into the belly first — let it soften and expand
- Continue the inhale into the ribcage — feel the sides widen
- Complete the inhale up into the chest
- Exhale from the chest... through the ribcage... back down through the belly

Think of filling a glass from the bottom up, and emptying it from the top down. Very slow. No force.

**Optional warming layer:**

As you inhale, imagine warmth spreading from your hands into your belly — like thawing from the inside out, very slowly.

**Note:** This is one of the gentlest practices. There are no contraindications. If you feel nothing during or after, that is completely okay — the nervous system will return in its own time. Do not push.

**Why it works:**

- Freeze is a shutdown response — the nervous system went quiet to protect you. Forcing it awake risks a re-freeze
- The three-part breath gently reanimates the body layer by layer, starting with the most primal — the belly — and moving upward
- The tactile self-touch at the beginning reactivates the sensory system through the safest possible input: your own hands

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

Freeze is not emptiness. It is protection. When something feels too much, the system can go quiet to survive.

The goal here is not to “feel more.” The goal is to return safely, slowly, without forcing.

**A few keys to notice**

- What happened right before I went numb or flat?
- Is there any sensation I *can* feel right now (temperature, weight, contact with the chair)?
- What would gentleness look like for the next hour?

**Small practice (1–2 minutes, after you finish listening)**

Choose one:

- Name 3 neutral sensations (for example: “feet on the floor,” “fabric on skin,” “air on face”).
- Or write one line: “Today, I don’t need to push. I can go slowly.”

*For deep freeze: try rubbing your hands together to generate warmth before you begin, or hold a warm drink against your palms. Sensation in the hands can be a gentle invitation back into the body.*

# **07  When Anger Rises and You Want to Tell Everyone Off**

*"Anger is not the enemy. Anger is the alarm. What you do with it — that's where the choice lives."*

**CONTEXT**

Irritation, sharpness, resentment, or rage are boiling up. There is an urge to lash out, snap, or say something that might feel satisfying for about ten seconds. The body is tense. The heat is rising. The inner voice is loud and not very kind — to yourself or anyone else.

**Key goal:** To give anger a safe, physical space to move through the body — without it moving into your relationships. And to return to clarity and the ability to choose your next move from a place of ground, not heat.

**When to use:**

- When you feel emotionally overheated
- Before sending a harsh message or saying something sharp
- When resentment has been building and needs a physical release
- Can also be practiced regularly to build the capacity to feel anger without being consumed by it

**THE BREATHING TECHNIQUE**

**Lion's Breath → 4–6 Stabilisation**

**Step 1 — Lion's Breath (6 rounds)**

Find a place where you feel comfortable making sound — or do a silent version (see note below).

- Take a deep inhale through the nose
- Open the mouth wide, stick the tongue out, and exhale forcefully with a strong 'HAAA'
- This is meant to feel intense and physical — that is the point. Do not be polite about it.
- Repeat 6 times. More if needed.

**Step 2 — 4–6 Stabilisation (2–3 minutes)**

- Soften the face. Let the jaw settle.
- Inhale through the nose for 4 counts
- Exhale slowly for 6 counts (mouth or nose)

If the heat rises again during 4–6, bring the sound back on the exhale. Let it move through.

**Note:** Lion's Breath is not recommended if you have a current eye, ear, or jaw injury. If you feel dizzy or very lightheaded, return to natural breathing immediately. Cold water on the face or wrists can help rapidly lower heart rate before you begin if activation is very high.
*If you are in a space where sound is not possible, the silent version (intense exhale with mouth wide open and tongue out, no voice) retains most of the effect — the physical movement matters more than the sound. The practice can be repeated whenever the heat rises again.*

**Why it works:**

- Anger is activation energy — it needs an outlet, not suppression. Lion's Breath gives it a sanctioned, physical exit through the face, throat and chest — the exact places anger tends to accumulate
- The forceful exhale discharges muscle tension faster than any cognitive reframing
- The transition to 4–6 brings the nervous system into regulation once the peak has passed — the sequence works because it honours the anger first, then settles it

### **A gentle psychological lens (optional)**

Anger is often the guardian of a boundary. It shows up when something feels unfair, unsafe, or too much.

Sometimes, though, anger doesn’t *feel* like anger. When it’s regularly swallowed — especially under cultural conditioning that teaches women to stay “nice,” quiet, or accommodating — it can turn inward and show up as **sadness, heaviness, numbness, or even depressive fog**.

So if you don’t identify as “an angry person,” this practice may still be one of the most valuable — because it helps your body release what was never allowed to be expressed.

This practice gives anger (expressed or unexpressed) a safe physical exit so it does not exit through words you regret — or through collapse.

**A few keys to notice**

- What boundary might be here?
- Where might I be holding anger I’m not admitting to (or not allowed to have)?
- If this feeling had a message, what would it be asking me to protect?

**Small practice (2–3 minutes, after you finish listening)**

Write two sentences:

- “What I want to say right now is: ___.” (no censoring)
- “What I want to say from ground is: ___.”
